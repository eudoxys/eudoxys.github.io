By David P. Chassin

Weather is the single most important exogenous driver of electric load in North America, and the growing electrification of space heating and transportation together with the rapid adoption of behind-the-meter (BTM) solar generation are strengthening that dependence. This report reviews weather-aware methods for two closely related problems: \emph{load estimation}, in which load that is unobserved, masked or confounded by weather must be reconstructed or normalized, and \emph{load forecasting}, in which future load is predicted given observed or forecast weather. 
    
The methods presently used by North American utilities, independent system operators (ISOs), regional transmission organizations (RTOs) and planning agencies are reviewed. These include degree-day and temperature-spline regression, statistically adjusted end-use (SAE) models, artificial neural network (ANN) short-term forecasters supplied by multiple weather vendors, weather-scenario simulation for 50/50 and 90/10 peak forecasts, change-point baseline models and weather-sensitive settlement profiles. 
    
This report then examines methods proposed by researchers that appear promising but have not yet been widely adopted, including probabilistic forecasting from numerical weather prediction (NWP) ensembles, gradient-boosted and deep sequence models, hierarchical reconciliation, net-load and BTM photovoltaic (PV) disaggregation, grey-box and physics-informed thermal models, climate-conditioned planning, machine-learning weather models and time-series foundation models. Each is assessed in light of the evidence available from North American data, the constraints of utility operations and the lessons of Winter Storms Uri (2021) and Elliott (2022). 
    
The report concludes with a discussion of the barriers to adoption and a research agenda focused on cold-weather extremes, electrification and calibrated uncertainty.
